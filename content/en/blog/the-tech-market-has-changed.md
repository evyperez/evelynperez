I've been working in tech for over 10 years. I've worked for companies in Brazil and the United States, and I'm currently based in Portugal. I wanted to share some of the changes I've noticed over the past few years. This is just my personal view, based on my own experience and what I've been seeing in the job market.

## Job opportunities have changed

Anyone who's worked in tech for a while probably remembers how active the job market used to be. Finding a new opportunity was relatively easy.

I went through a mass layoff in 2019, before the pandemic, and less than a week later, I was already starting a new job.

The market was so active that many people stayed at a company for less than a year before finding another opportunity with a much better salary. Job hoppers, people who frequently change jobs, became quite common during that time. That doesn't mean companies liked it, but there was so much demand for developers that switching jobs was relatively easy.

Today, things are very different.

Knowing how to code is no longer enough. Developers who only focus on implementation, without really understanding the code or the decisions behind it, are finding it harder to stand out.

As AI gets better at generating code, other skills are becoming more important, like planning tasks, reviewing code, understanding the product, and making architecture decisions.

After all, asking AI to write a feature is one thing. Understanding whether that code makes sense, is well structured, and will work within the rest of the project is another.

## Interviews have changed too

For a long time, algorithm interviews were extremely popular. What developer hasn't heard of *Cracking the Coding Interview*?

Usually, the process started with an HR interview, followed by a technical test, an algorithms interview, a conversation with a manager, and finally, one last interview.

Today, I've been seeing much longer hiring processes, AI interviews, and several rounds of assessments. And with so many people applying for the same position, especially remote roles, even getting an interview has become a challenge.

I've also noticed that soft skills are becoming more important. Being able to communicate, explain your decisions, and work well with others can matter just as much as knowing how to code.

It seems like companies are increasingly interested in understanding how developers think, make decisions, and work, rather than just whether they can solve a coding problem.

## Design has changed too

I've noticed a lot of changes in design as well. Knowing how to use Figma isn't as much of a differentiator anymore. I've even seen designers move away from Figma and start creating interfaces and components directly with Claude.

![Illustration of a hand holding a pencil over sheets of paper](/images/blog/claude-design.jpg "Claude Design logo")

I've also been seeing more Design Engineer roles, which combine design and development, especially when it comes to building interfaces and Design System components.

And with AI, the line between these areas is getting even smaller. Today, a designer can create functional interfaces without knowing much about programming. And a developer can create visually good interfaces without much design experience.

The thing is, I still don't think AI can replace human creativity. But it's getting better, and it can already create good interfaces, especially when someone knows how to guide it.

## The way we work has changed too

This is one of the changes I see as more negative. I've been working remotely since long before the pandemic. I spent many years freelancing and was lucky enough to work for companies that were very flexible.

During the pandemic, remote work became pretty much the standard for developers. And even afterward, many companies continued allowing it.

Back then, developers had more options. If a company required office attendance, it was relatively easy to find another one offering remote work. Companies also had to be more flexible to attract and keep developers.

Today, I've been seeing a lot more hybrid positions, and fully remote opportunities are much more competitive.

![People working at computers in a shared office](/images/blog/office-work.jpg "Photo by Israel Andrade on Unsplash")

I still prefer working remotely. After so many years working this way, I don't see much point in requiring people to be in an office when the work can be done from anywhere.

In my opinion, part of this pressure comes from companies and managers who still struggle to manage teams without having employees physically in the office.

## Not all changes are negative

Despite all these changes, I don't see AI as something entirely negative.

While competition has increased and companies have become more demanding, new opportunities have also appeared for people who know how to use these tools.

Today, it's much easier to learn something new, test an idea, or even build a product without needing an entire team.

A lot of people are afraid AI will take away jobs, but I also think it's helping people do things they couldn't do before, whether because they lacked technical knowledge, time, or money.

And I see that as something very positive.

But there's one thing that still concerns me: people who are just starting out.

If companies are increasingly looking for experienced professionals, and AI can already handle some of the tasks that junior developers used to do, how are those people supposed to gain experience?

After all, nobody starts out as a senior developer. Everyone needs an opportunity to learn, make mistakes, and gain experience working on real projects.

I don't know what the job market will look like in a few years, especially with how quickly AI is evolving. But I think we're still going to see a lot of changes, both positive and negative.

And I'd love to hear from others who also work in tech: have you noticed these changes? What has your experience been like?
